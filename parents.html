Start here

The sixty-second version

FIRST Tech Challenge — everyone says “FTC” — is a varsity sport for engineering. Each fall a new game is announced. A team of students has a few months to design, build, and program a robot roughly the size of a large microwave to play it, then competes head-to-head against other schools on a 12-foot field in front of a crowd, a referee, and a scoreboard.

Your son will not be handed a kit and told to follow instructions. He will be handed a problem nobody has solved yet, a team of peers, a deadline he cannot move, and a shop full of tools. Everything else — the CAD, the wiring, the Java, the strategy — gets taught by the students who learned it last year.

That is the part parents usually do not expect: this program is run by the students. Adults are moderators and mentors. They teach, supervise, drive the van, and keep everyone safe. They do not build the robot.

Not sure which program he joined?

Brophy runs three. FTC has five teams and is where most students start. FRC is the larger flagship team (991) with a bigger robot and a spring season. FLL is the LEGO-based program our students mentor for Loyola Academy scholars. Compare all three ›

What is FIRST Tech Challenge?

Four things to picture

If you have never seen a match, these are the four pieces that make one.

⛶

1. The game changes every year

Every September FIRST releases a brand-new game with a new field, new scoring elements, and a rule manual that runs well over a hundred pages. Last year’s robot is instantly obsolete. That reset is deliberate — nobody gets to coast on an old design, and a rookie joins on the same footing as a senior.

🤖

2. The robot has hard limits

It must start each match fitting inside an 18-inch cube, run on an approved battery and control system, and pass a safety inspection before it is allowed on the field. Within those limits, everything is a choice the students make and defend: the drivetrain, the arm, the intake, the code.

⏱

3. A match is two and a half minutes

Thirty seconds of autonomous, where the robot runs entirely on its own pre-written code and nobody may touch a controller. Then two minutes of driver-controlled play, ending with a frantic scoring period. Two robots form an alliance against two others, and your alliance partner changes almost every match.

🏆

4. Winning is not only about the score

The biggest awards in FTC go to the engineering notebook, the interview with judges, and how a team treats its competitors. The Inspire Award — the top honor — goes to the team that is strongest across the board, not the team with the highest score. A team can advance to state on judging alone.

The phrase you will hear all season: Gracious Professionalism™

It is a real, enforced part of FIRST culture, not a slogan on a poster. Teams lend each other parts mid-tournament. A team will help repair the robot that is about to play against them. Students are expected to compete hard and treat everyone well at the same time, and referees and judges genuinely score it. It is also the single thing parents most often tell us surprised them at their first event.

The shape of a year

What the season looks like from your kitchen calendar

The intensity is not flat. There are quiet weeks and there are weeks where you will not see him much. Knowing which is which ahead of time makes the whole thing easier.

Open the team calendar ›
  • August

    Preseason & onboarding

    Interest meetings, safety and tool training, CAD and programming bootcamps. Low commitment — this is the month to try it with no strings attached.

  • September

    Kickoff & design

    The new game is released. Teams read the manual, argue about strategy, prototype badly, and throw a lot of it away. Meetings pick up to two or three afternoons a week.

  • October – November

    Build & first competitions

    The busiest stretch. Fabrication, wiring, code, and driver practice, plus the first qualifying tournaments. Expect Saturdays and some later evenings.

  • December

    Iterate over break

    Rebuild the weak subsystem, rewrite autonomous, and get real practice hours in. The shop is open on a posted schedule, not every day.

  • January – February

    Championship push

    League finals and, for teams that advance, the Arizona State Championship. Meanwhile FRC 991 starts its own build season, and many FTC students jump in.

  • March – May

    Offseason

    Demos, outreach, summer camp prep, mentoring Loyola Academy FLL teams, and handing leadership to next year’s students.

The honest answer

How much time is this really?

Robotics is the rare activity where a student can genuinely dial the commitment up or down. These are the three tiers we actually see, so you can plan around the one that fits your family.

LevelWeekly timeWhat it looks likeGood fit for
Member 3–5 hrs One or two afternoon meetings a week, occasional Saturday, attends competitions as pit or scouting crew. Students with a full plate of sports, music, or theater who still want in.
Core builder 6–10 hrs Two or three afternoons plus most Saturdays in season. Owns a subsystem or a piece of the code. The most common tier — and the one where the learning really happens.
Lead / captain 12+ hrs Runs a subteam, manages the build schedule, writes the engineering notebook, mentors rookies, presents to judges. Juniors and seniors ready for real responsibility. This is a leadership job, not just a build job.

Peak weeks are real

In the two weeks before a tournament, and during the six-week FRC build season, expect late afternoons and full Saturdays. We publish the schedule in advance on the team calendar and in the weekly bulletin so it is never a surprise.

Academics come first, and we mean it

Grades are the condition of participation, not a competing priority. Students are expected to bring homework to long build days, and moderators will send a student to study hall over the shop. Tell us early if your son is struggling and we will adjust his load.

What your son takes away

Skills that outlast the trophy

The technical skills are real and transferable — several of them are the same tools used in professional engineering offices. But the ones alumni come back and mention are usually not technical.

Technical

  • Parametric CAD (Onshape)
  • Java programming and version control
  • Machining, fabrication, and shop safety
  • Wiring, sensors, and electrical debugging
  • Iterative design and testing

Human

  • Explaining your work to a judge
  • Disagreeing well with a teammate
  • Owning a deadline you cannot move
  • Teaching someone younger than you
  • Losing a match and staying gracious

Scholarships: FIRST maintains a scholarship program with tens of millions of dollars offered annually by participating colleges and companies. Being a FIRST alum is a real line on a college application, and our seniors get help writing about it. Scholarship links ›

Cost should never be the reason a student cannot join

If dues, a shirt, or a travel event would be a hardship, email the moderators. It is handled quietly and it happens every year. Nobody on the team knows who is on assistance, and it does not affect anything.

How you can help

You do not need an engineering degree

The most useful parent volunteers we have ever had were a nurse, an accountant, and a guy who owned a truck. Here is what actually moves the needle.

🍽

Feed the pit

Competition days start before dawn and run past dinner. A parent who organizes food for a tournament is worth their weight in aluminum.

🚚

Drive & haul

Robots, carts, spare parts, and tool chests have to get to the venue. Trucks, SUVs, and trailers are always needed.

💰

Open a door to a sponsor

If your employer has a matching-gift program, an engineering team, or a machine shop, that introduction is enormous.

📷

Shoot photos & video

Awards submissions and the engineering portfolio need documentation all season. Students forget; parents remember.

📝

Mentor a real skill

Welding, machining, business writing, graphic design, public speaking, accounting — all of it is used by this team.

👏

Just show up

Sit in the stands and cheer. Students notice. Bring earplugs — the venue is louder than you are expecting.

Your first tournament

What to expect on competition day

It looks like chaos. It is not. Here is the actual structure of the day so you know what you are watching.

  1. Load-in and inspection — early morning

    Teams set up a pit table and get the robot through hardware and software inspection. Expect a very early call time; students often arrive before 7 a.m.

  2. Judging interviews — morning

    Students present their season, their engineering notebook, and their outreach to a panel. Parents do not attend this part.

  3. Qualification matches — all day

    Each team plays several matches with rotating alliance partners. Between matches the pit crew repairs, adjusts, and re-tunes code. This is where most of the day goes.

  4. Alliance selection — afternoon

    Top-ranked teams pick partners for eliminations. It is genuinely tense and the best spectator moment of the day.

  5. Eliminations and awards — late afternoon

    Bracket play, then the awards ceremony — which is where advancement to the state championship is decided.

Bring to a tournament

  • Earplugs — it is loud
  • A water bottle and a light jacket (gyms are unpredictable)
  • Cash or a card for concessions
  • Patience for a long day; you may leave and come back

Spectator admission at Arizona qualifying events is typically free. Confirm on the event page.

Safety

Power tools and teenagers

It is a fair thing to ask about. Here is how the shop actually runs.

  • An adult moderator is present whenever the shop is open. Students never work alone.
  • Safety glasses are mandatory in the shop and in the pit, without exception.
  • Every student completes tool training before touching a machine, and training is tracked per tool.
  • Powered machining tools require direct adult supervision.
  • Batteries are charged and stored in a designated area with a posted procedure.
  • Every team has a designated student safety captain, and FIRST events run their own inspection and safety advisors.
  • School emergency and medical procedures apply in the shop and on all team travel.

Questions we get every August

Frequently asked

My son has zero experience. Is he behind?

No. Roughly half of every incoming class has never used a power tool or written code. The program is built to teach from zero — that is the whole point of the August bootcamps, and rookies are paired with returning students by design. The game resets every year anyway, so nobody starts with a finished answer.

Can he do robotics and a sport?

Yes, and many do. Tell the moderators about the conflict up front so he is assigned to a role that fits his schedule — scouting, programming, and business roles are especially flexible. The honest caveat is that a fall sport overlaps the busiest part of the FTC season, so his contribution will look different from a student in the shop every afternoon. That is fine and expected.

Is this only for kids who want to be engineers?

No. The team needs writers, photographers, presenters, business and sponsorship students, graphic designers, and strategists as much as it needs machinists. Some of our strongest members go on to study business, law, medicine, or the humanities, and the judged awards reward exactly those skills.

How do I know what is happening this week?

Three places, all public:

  • The team calendar — every meeting, build day, and competition.
  • The weekly bulletin — a short Monday summary of the week ahead.
  • The team email list — add your address here for schedule changes and competition-day logistics.
What is the difference between FTC and FRC again?

Scale, mostly. FTC robots fit in an 18-inch cube, cost less, and run a fall season with five small student-led teams. FRC robots are roughly the size and weight of a large vending machine, the season runs January through April with a famous six-week build, and Brophy fields a single large team (991). Many students do both: FTC in the fall, FRC in the spring. Full comparison ›

Does he need his own laptop, tools, or parts?

No. The team provides the robot, the control system, the tools, and the shop. Students use school or team laptops for CAD and programming. Personal safety glasses are welcome but we keep a supply.

Is there travel, and will I need to go?

Local qualifying tournaments are around the Phoenix metro area — day trips, and students travel together. Advancing to the state championship stays in Arizona. Travel beyond that is rare, announced far in advance, and comes with a fundraising plan. Parents are never required to travel, though you are always welcome in the stands.
